Weighted hydrographs for Montana brown trout

When does water help, by how much, and what do the forecasts imply?

The question

Reclamation and its partners can move water, but not much of it and not whenever they like. The operational question is not whether trout like water. It is when a given volume does the most good, and what the flow futures already in the models imply for populations that are managed on that basis.

Most flow-ecology work answers a related but different question, because it collapses the hydrograph before analysing it: summer mean discharge, peak magnitude, days below a threshold. Every one of those summaries is a hypothesis about when flow matters, chosen before looking at the data. This analysis does not collapse the hydrograph. It estimates a coefficient for every day of the year.

What a weighted hydrograph is

For each year i, with x_i(t) the standardized discharge anomaly on day t:

\[ y_i = a + \sum_{t=1}^{365} \beta(t)\, x_i(t) + \varepsilon_i \]

\(\beta(t)\) is the weighted hydrograph: how much a wet day on 3 August is worth compared with a wet day on 3 March. Score any hydrograph with it - an observed year, a forecast, a proposed release - and the sum is the predicted change in the response.

The seasonal-average model is a special case of this: \(\beta(t)\) constant inside the season and zero outside. So this framework can test whether a seasonal covariate was looking in the right place, instead of assuming it.

Read the gate before the figures. Every per-site page opens with it. The arithmetic produces a curve whether or not the record supports one; the gate is what separates a finding from a shape. A site that fails is a result about the resolution of Montana flow records, and it is reported as such rather than quietly dropped.

The honest limits

  • About 40 years against 365 days. Seasonal statements are usually supportable; single days often are not. Each site page reports the width of the best-day interval and says outright when a season is the finest honest claim.
  • The estimator can only build beta(t) from the leading modes of variation in flow, which are chosen without looking at the response. A signal living in a low-variance flow mode cannot appear in the curve, however real it is.
  • The gate tests prediction, not causation. A curve that passes predicts years the model did not see. It does not identify the mechanism, and the mechanism is what a flow recommendation ultimately rests on.
  • Scenario effects are measured against the observed gage climatology, not against the forecast model’s own historical run. Hydrologic model bias at a site is therefore reported as a climate effect.
